Notes from self-hosting my own infrastructure

What running Coolify, Docker, PostgreSQL and DNS by hand teaches that managed platforms never will — a field report from my own deployment practice.

  • DevOps
  • Self-hosting
  • Linux

There is a moment every self-hoster knows. You type the domain into the browser, the page fails to load, and you realise the cause could live in any one of five places: the registrar, the DNS resolver, the proxy, the container, or the application itself. Nothing tells you which. That moment — more than any feature — is why I self-host.

I run my own deployment environment on an Ubuntu host with Coolify: applications deploy from GitHub repositories into containers, a proxy layer terminates TLS and routes my configured domains, and PostgreSQL and Redis back the services that need them. It is not a production platform serving customers under an SLA. It is a personal workbench, and it has quietly reorganised how I think about software.

Managed platforms teach you the abstraction

I do not think managed platforms are bad — they are often the right engineering decision. But they teach you their interface, not the system underneath. Push to deploy, and a hundred deliberate decisions happen on your behalf: which ports open, how TLS is renewed, where logs land, what happens when a container dies at 3am. You learn the button. You do not learn the machine.

Operating the stack myself forced the questions into the open. When a deployment fails behind a proxy, the 502 you see is a symptom, never the cause. The cause is one layer deeper — in the container's exit logs, or the proxy's error log, or the application's own stdout. Nobody surfaces this for you. You develop a reading habit: container logs, then proxy logs, then application logs, in that order, every time.

DNS is where patience is manufactured

My hardest-won lesson had nothing to do with containers. DNS records propagate asynchronously, resolvers cache aggressively, and 'it works for me' is a meaningless sentence in a world where different resolvers hold different opinions about your domain. Early on, I reconfigured a perfectly correct setup out of impatience, breaking something that was quietly on its way to working.

Now I follow a fixed diagnostic order — registrar first, then resolver caches, then direct DNS queries, then the proxy — and I wait before changing anything. Boring discipline beats clever debugging. The DNS zone that resolves eventually and the one you broke in a hurry look identical from the outside.

Logs before restarts. One variable at a time. Boring is a feature you install in yourself.

The habits transfer everywhere

What surprised me most is how portable the discipline turned out to be. The habit of reading logs in sequence is the same skill as reading a stack trace properly. The instinct to change one variable at a time is the same instinct that makes debugging scientific instead of superstitious. The reflex to verify each layer — locally, then through the firewall, then from outside — is just the scientific method wearing an operations uniform.

  • Logs before restarts — a restart destroys the evidence you need.
  • One variable at a time — two changes teach you nothing about either.
  • Verify layer by layer — every failing layer will announce itself if you ask in order.
  • Backups you haven't restored are superstition, not safety.

Would I recommend it?

If you are a student of systems — of how the web actually delivers a request from a URL to a process and back — self-hosting is the most honest education available. You will lose evenings to DNS. You will learn what a reverse proxy actually does because you configured its routing yourself. And the first time a service you deployed stays up for a month, quietly serving requests through infrastructure you understand end-to-end, something clicks that no tutorial can manufacture.

Start small: one host, one service, one domain. Add PostgreSQL when something needs it, Redis when something needs that. Let the infrastructure be pulled into existence by real projects rather than pushed into shape by enthusiasm. That is how mine grew, and it is the only way I would recommend growing one.
